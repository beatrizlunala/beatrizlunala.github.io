Neighbor

An App for Farmers Horse Coffee

Project Overview

Neighbor is a mobile app concept designed to enhance the experience at Farmers Horse Coffee (FHC), a beloved local cafe near Northeastern University's campus. Through extensive user research and interviews, this project explores how digital tools can complement—rather than replace—the intimate, "lived-in" atmosphere that makes FHC special.

Research Questions: How Might We...

  • ...preserve the work-social balance of a smaller, more intimate cafe without having one group of people overbearing the other?
  • ...encourage social interaction without disrupting the work-focused atmosphere?
  • ...help users discover and engage with the 'intimate' community elements that create belonging?
  • ...help new visitors understand what to expect from Farmers Horse before their first visit?
  • ...preserve the spontaneous, exploratory feeling while providing useful logistical information?
  • ...help users transition between work mode and social mode within the same visit?
  • ...surface the "parallel play" motivation that makes shared workspaces productive?

About Farmers Horse Coffee

Farmers Horse Coffee is a small, intimate cafe located across from the Massachusetts Avenue Orange Line station in Boston. Its cozy atmosphere, customer graffiti-covered walls, community board games, and carefully curated interior make it a hot spot for students and working adults who want to spend hours there—whether catching up with an old friend or grinding out remote work.

What Makes It Special?

Unlike chain cafes that feel transactional, Farmers Horse has a distinct "lived-in" character. The space features:

  • Customer writings and drawings covering the walls (some dating back to 2017)
  • Well-used board games available for patrons
  • A mix of communal tables and cozy couch seating
  • An outdoor patio area on Mass Ave
  • A small free library box outside

Research Process

Phase 1: Initial Exploration

Documented three potential locations, ultimately selecting Farmers Horse Coffee based on its unique community dynamics and dual-purpose usage (work vs. social).

Phase 2: Deep Dive Observation

Conducted extensive observation at different times of day and days of the week to understand usage patterns, peak hours, and how the space transforms based on context.

Phase 3: User Interviews

Interviewed four regular patrons to understand motivations, pain points, and what keeps them coming back to Farmers Horse over other cafes.

Phase 4: Analysis & Synthesis

Identified key themes and developed revised "How Might We" questions to guide the design direction.

Interview Methodology

  • Format: Semi-structured interviews lasting approximately 30 minutes
  • Locations: Three in-person (two on campus, one at Farmers Horse), one via Zoom
  • Recording: All interviews were recorded (with consent) and transcribed for analysis
  • Criteria: Participants had to have visited Farmers Horse at least once, with preference for "regulars" (past or present)
  • Demographics: Primarily college students with interest or experience in Design or Computer Science

Key Findings

1. Dual-Purpose Tensions

The space successfully accommodates both focused work and social interaction, but these modes coexist rather than conflict. Users appreciate the "parallel play" dynamic—working individually while surrounded by others doing the same.

"If everyone is kind of also doing the same thing, there's definitely a little bit of that motivation... It's a parallel play kind of thing." — Interviewee Two

2. Physical Constraints Create User Friction

Limited seating, long lines that extend out the door, lack of AC in summer, and inconsistent power outlet availability are significant pain points. However, users rated these issues as 4-5 out of 5 in terms of impact, meaning they often choose alternative cafes when these constraints are present.

"It was so busy we ended up going to Cafe Bene. So, like, I guess that was, like an impact... but we were still satisfied. Farmers Horse was more of a means to an end." — Interviewee Two

3. The "Lived-In" Character Creates Belonging Without Direct Interaction

The graffiti walls, worn board games, and community bulletin board create a sense of connection to past visitors without requiring face-to-face interaction. This passive participation in the space's history is deeply meaningful to users.

"It feels like an inviting space. More of a neighborhood cafe, as opposed to chain cafes like Pavement." — Interviewee One
"I think about those [graffiti messages] sometimes... we were existing in the same spot, doing completely different things... It's kind of cool to be a part of that, this shared experience." — Interviewee Four

4. Generational Barriers to Social Connection

Multiple participants expressed desire for social connection but felt constrained by unspoken social norms that make approaching strangers feel awkward or inappropriate. This is perceived as a generational issue rather than a space-specific problem.

"I feel like no one in our age group is, like, willingly approaching a stranger to become friends, unfortunately... it's, like, kind of strange nowadays to, like, approach someone" — Interviewee Three

Interesting Note: When asked about indicators of openness to conversation, one participant (Interviewee Four) suggested that the space itself should signal what type of experience to expect, rather than individual indicators. This reveals tension between wanting connection and wanting it to feel natural rather than forced.

5. Time and Context Shape the Experience

The cafe's atmosphere varies significantly based on time of day and day of week. Weekends and Fridays trend more social, while weekdays lean toward focused work. Users strategically plan visits around these patterns.

"I would say, less with the times that I've noticed, more with the days of the week, that it's different... if it's on a weekend or a Friday... people are chatting, hanging out... but other times of the week, it's more of focusing" — Interviewee Two
"It depends on the type of work that I'm doing... if I'm ever going there to do work, it's specifically work that it's okay if I don't really finish" — Interviewee Two

Design Insights

What I Initially Assumed

I thought Farmers Horse's main challenge was balancing work and social atmospheres equally—that there might be tension between people who wanted quiet focus and those who wanted to socialize.

What I Actually Discovered

  • Physical constraints matter more than atmospheric ones: Seating availability, line length, and comfort issues are bigger barriers to returning than noise or social dynamics.
  • The "lived-in" aesthetic creates community feeling without direct interaction: People feel connected through shared space and history, not necessarily through conversation.
  • Context is everything: Users self-select visit times based on their needs, and the space naturally adapts to accommodate different purposes at different times.
  • Parallel play is a feature, not a bug: Working alongside others provides motivation and accountability without the pressure of socializing.

Revised "How Might We" Questions

Based on these findings, I've refined my research questions to focus on:

  • Helping new visitors understand what to expect before their first visit
  • Preserving the spontaneous, exploratory feeling while providing useful logistical information
  • Helping users transition between work mode and social mode within the same visit
  • Surfacing the "parallel play" motivation that makes shared workspaces productive

Next Steps

With research complete and insights synthesized, the next phase involves:

  • Sketching initial app concepts that address the refined "How Might We" questions
  • Creating low-fidelity prototypes for three distinct app ideas
  • Testing concepts with target users to validate assumptions
  • Iterating based on feedback
  • Developing a high-fidelity prototype of the most promising concept

Stay tuned for updates as this project evolves!
